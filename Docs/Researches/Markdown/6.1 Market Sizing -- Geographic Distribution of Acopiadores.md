# Acopiadores de Granos in Argentina: Market Size, Geographic Distribution & Structure (2024–2026)

## Executive Summary

Argentina's grain storage and collection sector (acopio) is a critical link in the country's agricultural supply chain, handling roughly 45% of the internal commercialization of grains from a national production of approximately 130–136 million tons per year. The sector comprises around **950–1,000 independent private acopiadores** organized through the Federación de Acopiadores de Cereales, alongside approximately 155 cooperatives and a handful of multinational exporters with their own storage networks. These operators collectively manage 1,600–2,500 storage plants and approximately 38 million tons of fixed storage capacity, supplemented by 50–55 million tons stored in silobolsa technology. The market shows strong geographic concentration in the Pampas region (88% of plants), with Buenos Aires, Santa Fe, and Córdoba accounting for the vast majority of operations. The sector is experiencing a gradual consolidation, with independent SMB acopiadores losing volume to large cooperatives and multinational exporters conducting direct purchasing from producers.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8][^9]

***

## Total Number of Acopiadores

### Overall Market Count

The most comprehensive data comes from the now-superseded Registro Único de Operadores de la Cadena Agroindustrial (RUCA), which was migrated to the SISA system in May 2025. According to Bolsa de Comercio de Rosario (BCR) reports, Argentina had approximately **1,231–1,259 registered "acopiador-consignatario" enterprises** operating **2,380–2,458 storage plants** across the country. A separate SAGyP count, which included plants with overlapping registrations, recorded 2,151 acopiador-consignatario plants (and up to 3,306 when counting duplicities).[^2][^10][^11][^12][^8][^9]

### Breakdown by Operator Type

| Operator Type | Number of Companies | Number of Plants | % of Plants | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Independent private acopiadores** (SA, SRL) | ~1,073 | ~1,622 | ~68% | Family-owned SMBs; target market[^8] |
| **Cooperatives** (1st and 2nd degree) | ~155 | ~476–701 | ~20–30% | Includes ACA, AFA, CAFER[^8][^9] |
| **Exporters with own acopio** | ~10 major firms | ~298 | ~13% | Cargill, COFCO, Bunge, Viterra, LDC, etc.[^9] |
| **Corredores (brokers) with some acopio** | ~287 registered | Minimal | Marginal | Mostly intermediaries, not plant operators[^13][^14] |

The Federación de Centros y Entidades Gremiales de Acopiadores de Cereales—the principal trade association for private acopiadores—reports approximately **950 member companies**, collectively representing USD 3 billion in investment, 40% of interior storage installations, and serving 70% of the country's producers. These private firms commercialize approximately 50% of the nation's grain, distribute 40% of phytosanitary products, and 25% of agrochemicals.[^5][^15]

### Cooperatives with Acopio Operations

The cooperative system plays an outsized role. The Asociación de Cooperativas Argentinas (ACA)—a second-degree cooperative grouping over 140 member cooperatives and 50,000 producers—recorded a historic 25 million tons of grain in the 2024/25 campaign, making it the single largest acopio operator in Argentina. Agricultores Federados Argentinos (AFA), with 36,000 associated producers, 26 cooperative centers, 1,600+ permanent employees, and 123 registered plants, handles approximately 5 million tons per year.[^16][^17][^9]

### Exporters with Own Acopio Networks

Major multinational grain traders operate their own inland storage networks to "originate" grain directly. In 2025, the top 10 exporters concentrated 90.5% of the 105.5 million tons of grain and subproducts registered for export. Key players include:[^18][^19]

- **Cargill**: 59 registered acopio plants; originates ~70% of its exports through own network; 17.3 Mt exported in 2025[^9][^18]
- **COFCO**: ~10 plants (inherited from Nidera and Noble); originates >50% of its exports; ~12.3 Mt exported[^17][^18]
- **Viterra/SAMSA**: ~4 Mt acopio per year, operates both directly and through Lartirigoyen[^17]
- **Bunge**: 7 plants; ~3 Mt acopio; mobilizes via truck, rail, and barge[^9][^17]
- **Louis Dreyfus**: 11 plants; ~3 Mt acopio[^17][^9]

### Corredores (Brokers)

Argentina counts 287 registered grain brokerage firms, though these are predominantly intermediaries rather than storage operators. Buenos Aires hosts ~49% of these (especially in CABA), Santa Fe ~41% (concentrated in Rosario), Córdoba ~4%, and Entre Ríos ~3%. A hybrid "correacopio" model has emerged in recent years, where some brokers also engage in storage and origination activities, blurring traditional roles.[^20][^13][^14][^21]

## Geographic Distribution by Province

### Regional Concentration

The Pampas region (Buenos Aires, Santa Fe, Córdoba, Entre Ríos, and La Pampa) concentrates **88% of all acopio plants** in Argentina, mirroring the fact that this area produces roughly 90% of the country's total grain output.[^3][^2]

Buenos Aires province alone accounts for approximately **40% of all storage plants** in the country. The SAGyP Informe Especial on storage also confirmed this dominance, noting that the high share is amplified by significant on-farm storage capacity, especially in the center-south wheat belt.[^2]

The most recent official figures place Argentina's total commercial fixed storage capacity at 75–80 million tons, but this includes industrial and port terminal facilities beyond primary acopio plants.[^4]

### Córdoba

Based on the Federación's cross-reference with URGARA, approximately **81 associated private acopiadores** operate in Córdoba province. The Sociedad de Acopiadores de Granos de la Provincia de Córdoba is the leading provincial entity, affiliated to the national Federación. Córdoba presents the most critical storage deficit in the country relative to its production volume, with the San Francisco area being a particularly acute example of mismatch between production and storage capacity.[^23][^25][^26][^27][^28]

### Santa Fe

Approximately **58 associated private acopiadores** are members of the Federación in Santa Fe. However, the province's importance is amplified by the Gran Rosario port complex: 29 port terminals along 70 km of the Paraná River, of which 22 handle grains, with 12 having oilseed processing facilities. The Gran Rosario area alone holds approximately 15% of the country's commercial fixed storage capacity and 74% of port storage. In 2024, Gran Rosario handled 67.7 Mt of agro-industrial products, representing 76% of all national dispatches.[^29][^28][^30]

### Buenos Aires

Buenos Aires hosts approximately **96 associated private acopiadores** through the Federación—the largest contingent of any province. Key port infrastructure includes Bahía Blanca/Ingeniero White (817,000 tons terminal capacity, 5 plants), Quequén (568,350 tons, 4 plants), and Zárate (391,000 tons, 3 plants). The interior includes important acopio hubs around Pergamino, Junín, and the northern Buenos Aires grain belt.[^10][^28]

### Entre Ríos and La Pampa

Entre Ríos has approximately **27 associated private acopiadores** through the Federación and about 2.1 Mt of storage capacity. La Pampa, with ~1.2 Mt capacity and approximately 3% of national acopio, concentrates its plants in the northeastern departments (Chapaleufú, Quemú Quemú, Maracó, Conhello) and is served by the Centro de Acopiadores de Cereales de La Pampa y Limítrofes based in General Pico.[^31][^28][^24]

## Size Distribution of Acopiadores

### Storage Capacity Distribution

A survey by the Federación de Acopiadores de Cereales found that member companies average **2.15 plants per company**, with an average storage capacity per plant of **31,800 tons**, giving a total sector capacity of approximately 24 million tons for the private acopio sector alone.[^32]

| Size Category | % of Companies | Capacity per Company |
|---|---|---|
| Small | 60% | <20,000 tons[^32] |
| Medium | 32% | 20,000–80,000 tons[^32] |
| Large | 8% | >80,000 tons[^32] |

At the plant level, BCR data from RUCA registrations shows:[^8]

| Plant Capacity | Total Plants | Private Plants |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 2,000 t | 590 | 180 |
| 2,001–5,000 t | 599 | 483 |
| 5,001–10,000 t | 225 | 412 |

| **Total** | **2,458** | **1,622** |

### Employment

The Federación's survey revealed the following employee distribution among member companies:[^33][^34]

| Employees | % of Companies |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | 39% |
| 11–20 | 33% |
| 21–30 | 8% |
| 31–40 | 2% |
| 41+ | 17% |

The sector-wide average is approximately **26 employees per company**, totaling about **20,000 direct jobs** across the private acopio sector. A typical small acopiador (comprising 39% of firms) operates with 1–10 employees, while mid-sized operators (33%) have 11–20. By comparison, examples from the top 12 ranking show larger family-owned firms like Gaviglio Comercial (7 plants, ~200 employees) and Tomás Hnos (6 plants, 230 employees).[^33][^17]

### Fleet and Operations

About 65% of acopiadores maintain their own truck fleet (averaging 6 vehicles per company), for a sector total of approximately 3,000 trucks. Railway usage is minimal—under 10% of firms use rail, accounting for only ~6% of grain transported. Approximately 55% of acopiadores also operate their own agricultural production, averaging ~4,000 hectares each, with a combined cultivated area exceeding 1.8 million hectares.[^32]

### Typical Revenue

Specific revenue data for individual acopiadores is not publicly available. Revenue derives from: (a) conditioning services (drying, screening, fumigation), (b) the buy-sell margin between producer purchase price and sale price to exporters/industry, (c) storage fees, (d) freight services, and (e) the "blending benefit" from cereal mixing. The Federación estimates total sector investment at over USD 3 billion.[^15][^5][^8]

## Data Sources for an Acopiador Census

### ARCA / SISA Registry (formerly RUCA)

As of May 2025, grain operator registration migrated from RUCA to the **SISA (Sistema de Información Simplificado Agrícola)** managed by ARCA (formerly AFIP). The new system consolidates plant registration and operator categories, with acopiador-consignatario being one of the defined activities requiring plant registration. ARCA/SISA publishes a **public consultation of active plants** ("Registro Consulta Pública Plantas activas"), though the data is primarily structured for regulatory compliance rather than as a commercial database. Registration is now free and indefinite, following the elimination of fees and simplification enacted by Resolution 82/2024.[^35][^36][^11][^12]

### Bolsa de Cereales Registration Databases

The **Bolsa de Comercio de Rosario (BCR)** maintains the most detailed analytical data on acopiadores. Its research division (Dirección de Informaciones y Estudios Económicos) has produced the foundational reports characterizing the structure of the sector, including operator counts, plant sizes, and provincial distributions. The **Bolsa de Cereales de Buenos Aires** publishes annual reports (Memoria e Informe) with related market statistics. The **Bolsa de Cereales de Córdoba (BCCBA)** provides regional grain data and pizarra (reference) prices.[^37][^38][^8][^9]

### Federación de Acopiadores de Cereales

The **Federación de Centros y Entidades Gremiales de Acopiadores de Cereales** (commonly Federación de Acopiadores) is the principal trade association, grouping approximately 950 private acopiador companies through regional centers (Centros de Acopiadores). Provincial chapters include:[^5][^15]

- Sociedad de Acopiadores de Granos de la Provincia de Córdoba[^26]
- Sociedad Gremial de Acopiadores de Granos de Rosario[^39]
- Centro de Acopiadores de Cereales de La Pampa y Limítrofes[^31]
- Cámara Gremial de Recibidores y Entregadores de Cereales de Buenos Aires (CGREC)[^40]

Membership data is not publicly available in bulk but can be approximated from URGARA cross-checks and Federación publications.[^28]

### SAGyP Open Data

The Secretaría de Agricultura, Ganadería y Pesca publishes **monthly physical grain stock data** from the Registro Sistémico de Movimientos y Existencias de Granos, covering inventories in acopio, industrial, and export plants but excluding on-farm stocks. The **datos.gob.ar** portal hosts a "Granos – Centros de acopio" dataset providing departmental-level plant counts by management mode. The SAGyP infrastructure reports have historically provided capacity data by province and operator type.[^41][^42][^43][^44][^10]

### INTA Academic Research

The most rigorous academic study is the INTA Balcarce "Radiografía del sistema de acopio de granos en Argentina" (Abadía, Urcola, Ferrari & Bartosik, 2020), which surveyed 86 plants in the core production region and identified three clusters (large, intermediate, and small) differing in capacity, reception rate, rotation index, and fumigation practices. The study found that large and intermediate plants handled 95% of grain volume, while small plants faced significant equipment limitations.[^45][^46]

## Market Concentration

### Top Operator Dominance

The top 12 acopios in Argentina—including ACA, Cargill, COFCO, AFA, AGD, Viterra/SAMSA, Louis Dreyfus, Bunge, Lartirigoyen, Los Grobo, Tomás Hnos, and Gaviglio—collectively commercialize over **60 million tons** of grain per year, accounting for roughly 45% of the country's internal grain trade.[^1][^17]

The **top 17 companies** by number of registered plants hold 440 plants, representing **18% of all registered acopio plants** nationally. The cooperative movement (all cooperative-registered plants combined) holds approximately **30% of registered plants**, while the 10 largest exporters control about **13%** of registered plants.[^10][^9]

### Independent SMB Share

Independent private acopiadores (non-cooperative, non-exporter) represent the largest share numerically: approximately **1,073 companies operating 1,622 plants (~68% of total plants)**. However, their share of total volume is smaller than their plant count suggests, as many operate small facilities. The Federación estimates its members' combined capacity at approximately **24 million tons** out of a national commercial fixed total of 75–80 Mt, or roughly **30–32%** of total commercial storage.[^4][^8][^32]

### Consolidation Trends

The sector has been experiencing gradual consolidation since the early 2000s:[^9]

- **Fewer small producers, less acopio traffic**: Approximately 100,000 agricultural operations disappeared between the 1988 and 2008 censuses. Small landowners increasingly rent their fields to large operators who often sell directly to exporters, bypassing local acopiadores.[^9]
- **Direct purchasing by exporters**: Large multinational traders have expanded direct origination, sometimes without fixed acopio infrastructure, reducing the intermediation role of independent acopiadores.[^20][^9]
- **Cooperative absorption**: Some cases of small acopiadores being purchased by cooperatives or larger operators have been documented, though the overall number of registered plants has remained relatively stable over the past decade.[^9]
- **"Correacopio" hybrids**: Strategic alliances between brokers and acopiadores are emerging to close the price differential that larger cooperatives can offer through their scale advantage.[^9]
- **Export concentration intensifying**: In 2025, just 10 companies accounted for 90.5% of all grain export registrations, up from similar levels in 2024. This suggests that downstream concentration is squeezing margins for mid-chain operators.[^19][^18]

### Silobolsa as an Equalizer

The proliferation of silobolsa technology has partially offset the structural disadvantages of small acopiadores. More than 40% of Argentina's annual production (50–55 Mt) is now stored in silobolsa, providing flexible, low-cost capacity that adapts to all production scales. This Argentine innovation, exported to over 50 countries, allows both producers and small acopiadores to increase their storage capacity without heavy fixed investment.[^47][^7][^48]

***

## Key Data Limitations

Several important caveats apply to the data presented in this report:

- **No single centralized census exists** that cleanly separates independent private acopiadores from cooperatives and exporter-owned facilities within the same registration categories.
- The **RUCA/SISA registration counts** include all operator types under the "acopiador-consignatario" heading, requiring manual decomposition by legal form.
- **Storage capacity figures** for provincial distribution rely on older estimates (2008–2016 vintage); updated granular data by province and operator type has not been publicly released in recent years.
- **Revenue data** for individual acopiadores is not systematically collected or published.
- The transition from **RUCA to SISA** in May 2025 may have resulted in temporary data gaps as operators were migrated and pending applications were voided.[^11][^12]
- The **INTA Radiografía study** (2020) is the best available typological analysis but was based on a sample of 86 plants in the core region only.[^45]
